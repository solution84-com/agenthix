The redesign — pages for HQ and the sites, and doing things where you look

Agreed with the owner on 2026-10-16, after reviewing the built Fleet Console live, and built that day. It replaces the earlier boards for the shell, Fleet, a site's page, HQ's site, Scheduled and the notices, each marked as superseded where it stands. Spec §6, §7; ADR-0120. Maintenance has its own board, Plan maintenance.

What this is

  • Ten decisions, each with what it is, then why. The mockups follow the Light/Dark switch.
  • Where the build left something out, the decision says so under Left out when built.

Before

  • Notices sat in a list under the bell that could not be scrolled, opened or dismissed one by one; Activity mixed them with the record.
  • Only a card's title was a link.
  • Scheduling or backing up meant Fleet → the machine → a menu, three levels deep; the Scheduled and Backups pages only listed.
  • One page mixed HQ, its site and its machine; a site and its machine had two names, so a move read office-server · on new-server.

1HQ has its own page; a site and its machine are one thing, named after the machine

A machine runs one site, so the Fleet Console shows them as one thing with the machine's name — "office-server", tagged Site. When a site moves or is restored onto another machine, it takes that machine's name: "office-server on new-server" can no longer happen. Its page has the site first, then the machine below. HQ is separate: its own page and its own place at the top of the menu, apart from the sites.

Why the HQ page shows its machine

Someone looking at HQ wants to know two things: can people use it (health, address, version, backups) — and if not, why. The "why" is usually the machine: a full disk or no memory takes HQ down. So the machine sits below HQ, on the same page, as it does below a site.

HQ's own site is only a line here, with a link — runs and capacity are about agents, not about HQ, so they live on the site's page under Sites. That keeps the HQ page about HQ, which was the point of separating it.

Why

One name for one thing. People never have to hold two names for what is, to them, one box doing one job. Following the machine's name means the name is what they see on the hardware, in its terminal and in its status — and a move can't leave a misleading name behind.

HQ is different in kind — what people sign in to, with an address, a front door and the power to suspend or erase everything — so it gets its own page and the top of the menu. That removes the mix the old page had (HQ, site and machine at once).

The site comes first, the machine below, because most visits are about whether agents can run; the hardware is the cause you look into next, on the same page.

Consequence: a site's name changes when it moves or is restored (HQ's projects then show the new name). Before the first release this is a straight change, nothing to convert.

Left out when built: a site's Projects here (the Fleet Console has no list of a site's projects) and HQ's data size under Who depends on it (no figure reports it). The Logs button opens a page that says it comes later.

2Every action is a button on the thing's page — never in a menu

The header of each page lists everything you can do to that thing as buttons (see the mockups in 1). No "more" menu, no action reachable only from somewhere else. Maintenance on a thing is one button, Plan maintenance…, which opens the panel with that thing chosen (Plan maintenance).

Why

People recognise what they can see; they don't remember what is hidden (recognition over recall). A hidden menu three levels deep is a recall problem: you had to know the action existed and where.

The header answers "what can I do here?" in one glance: the buttons are the whole list. Four to six buttons can be read at once.

Cost: a header gets busy when a thing has many actions — which is why proposal 1 keeps each page to one thing.

3A whole card is the link

Anywhere a card shows a thing, pressing anywhere on it opens that thing's page. It highlights on hover and on keyboard focus. There are no other links inside a card.

Why

A card looks like one object, so people press it like one. When only the title is a link, the card promises a click and the pointer finds none.

A bigger target is faster and surer to hit (Fitts's law), with a mouse and on a phone.

Nothing competes for the click. With no links inside, there is one obvious thing the card does.

Cost: a card can't hold a second action — fine, its job is to take you to the thing, where every action is. A row that has its own buttons (Back up now on Backups) keeps them beside the link, outside it.

4Two pages named after jobs — Maintenance and Backups — and each can start its job

Maintenance lists what is planned and when, with + Plan maintenance. Backups lists HQ and every site with Back up now and Export… on each row, and Restore a lost site…. Each opens the same form as the button on the thing's own page.

Why

People start in two ways, and both should work. From the thing — "this site needs more runs" — its page has the button. From the job — "what's planned this weekend?", "is everything backed up?" — the job's page has the list and the button. Before, the job pages only listed, so starting from the job was a dead end.

A page should be able to do what its name says. On a page called Scheduled, the first thing people tried was to schedule something.

One form, two doors. Both buttons open the same form, so it is learned once and behaves the same wherever you start.

Cost: two entry points to keep in step — one shared form keeps them the same.

5Call it "Maintenance", not "Scheduled"

The page covers work done now, work planned for later, and work done recently, on HQ and every site.

Why

Name the job, not the timing. People decide what to do first and when second. "Scheduled" only covers later, so work done now had nowhere to show.

"Change" was tried next and was the wrong word: what a fleet admin does here is maintenance — upgrading, moving, giving a site room, suspending. Members read the same word: Scheduled maintenance.

It is also where to look when something went wrong — "what was done?" is the first question after a problem.

Called Dashboard, not Fleet: it is the overview of everything. What needs you on top. Then the fleet view — HQ as the hub with its sites drawn hanging from it, so it reads as "HQ has these sites" — and the totals. Each card opens its own page.

Why

"Dashboard" says what the page is — the overview you open first — without a term to learn. "Fleet" becomes the name of one view on it (HQ and its sites), so the word keeps a single meaning.

The picture is the model. Sites belong to HQ — they take their work from it and stop when it stops. Drawing them hanging from HQ teaches that without words, and shows at once what a problem with HQ affects.

One glance answers "is everything fine?" — the problems first, then every thing with its state, then the fleet's totals. A dashboard is the page people return to; the other pages are where they go to act.

Cards still open their page (proposal 3), so the dashboard is also the way in.

Cost: with many sites the picture gets long — past twenty, the sites become a compact list under HQ, with a search. Machines that left the fleet are listed after the totals, under Left the fleet, until a fleet admin removes them.

7The menu in three parts: Dashboard and HQ on top, the sites in the middle (scrolls), the jobs pinned at the bottom

Top, fixed: Dashboard, HQ. Middle, scrolls, no limit: Sites — a Find a site field, then every site with a dot for its state, its state in words beside the dot for screen readers and on hover. Bottom, always visible: Maintenance, Backups, Activity, Fleet admins, Remote access (only with Cloud), Help, and you. HQ is not above the sites as a parent, and no page shows a trail like "Fleet › HQ › office-server": HQ and each site are reached from the menu or the dashboard.

Why

HQ and sites are different kinds of thing, so they sit apart: HQ is one and always there, so it is fixed at the top; sites are many, so they get the part that scrolls.

The sites can grow without pushing the jobs out of reach. However many there are, Maintenance and Backups are in the same place on every page — people find them by position, without looking.

Every site is one click from anywhere, and its dot shows a problem even while you are on another page.

Sites keep a steady order (HQ's site first, then by name), so a site is always where you last saw it; problems show by their dot, not by jumping to the top.

Cost: on a short screen the middle is small — the bottom stays one compact block, and Find a site gets you there by typing.

8HQ's own site is a site like the others, named after HQ's machine

"hq-box", tagged HQ's site: first among the sites in the menu and on the dashboard, with its own site page. HQ's page has one line about it with a link. The machine shows on both pages, since both run on it. It offers only a change of capacity in Plan maintenance: it upgrades and moves with HQ.

Why

One answer to "where do agents run?" — every site under HQ, its own included, so nobody learns an exception or looks in two places.

9Help — the guide inside the Fleet Console, and a link from each section

Help at the foot of the menu opens the user guide's pages for running Agenthix, with every command explained where it is used. Each section on a page has a small How this works link to its part of the guide. Screens show a command only when you must run it now; the rest live in the guide.

Why

Many new ideas, met rarely. A fleet admin opens the Fleet Console now and then, often under pressure. Help that is one click away, in the words of the screen, saves them leaving to search.

Help at the point of need. A link beside the section you're puzzling over beats a manual you have to navigate; the menu entry is for reading ahead.

Screens stay short. Commands and explanations move to the guide, so pages show the state and the action — which is what makes them quick to read.

Kept with the Fleet Console, not on a website: it is reached on the machine alone, may have no internet, and lets nothing in from elsewhere; the guide is built into it, the same version as the screens, and drawn as text, never as HTML.

Cost: the guide's running pages are written for each idea the screens use. HQ has no Help yet — the same entry there would be consistent.

10Notices in a panel beside the menu; Activity is the record

The bell opens the notices in a panel pinned beside the menu, as in HQ, over the page. Dismiss all is at the top; each notice opens what it is about — a site, HQ, a move's outcome, Fleet admins — and has its own Dismiss beside it. Activity is only the record of what fleet admins and machines did, newest first, kept in HQ for a year: filtered by who, what it was done to and its kind, with nothing on it to dismiss or change.
